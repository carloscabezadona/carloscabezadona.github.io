The Industrial Accelerator Act (IAA) aims to use public money to create demand for European-made, low-carbon products. At the Competitiveness Council on 24 September, ministers did not hold a full debate on it, because there had not been enough progress. The sticking point is a deceptively simple question: which countries count as ‘Europe’ for the purposes of the European preference?

## What the Commission proposes

The Commission tabled the proposal on [4 March 2026](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/qanda_26_516) (COM(2026) 100, procedure [2026/0068(COD)](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/legislative-train/theme-a-new-plan-for-europe-s-sustainable-prosperity-and-competitiveness/file-industrial-decarbonisation-accelerator-act)). It is a regulation that puts into practice one of the ideas of the Draghi report: using public procurement, auctions and support schemes as ‘lead markets’ for clean and strategic products. The stated goal is to raise manufacturing from 14.3% of EU GDP in 2024 to 20% by 2035.

According to the [Commission's questions and answers](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/qanda_26_516), the proposal has four building blocks:

| Block | Content |
|---|---|
| Energy-intensive industries | Low-carbon requirements for steel used in automotive and construction; ‘Made in EU’ and low-carbon requirements for cement and aluminium where public procurement or public support is involved. Chemicals at a later stage. |
| Clean technologies and automotive | ‘Made in EU’ requirements for batteries, storage, solar PV, heat pumps, wind, electrolysers and nuclear technologies in certain tenders, auctions and support schemes, and for electric vehicles and their components. |
| Foreign investment | Conditions for investments above €100 million by companies from countries holding more than 40% of global capacity in EVs, batteries, solar or critical raw materials: EU majority shareholding, technology transfer, integration into EU value chains and job creation. |
| Permitting | Digital procedures, single points of contact, maximum timelines of 18 months for certain projects, and ‘industrial acceleration areas’ designated by Member States. |

The key to understanding the negotiation is that, in the proposal, ‘Made in EU’ does not mean the EU-27 alone. The Commission would give treatment equivalent to EU origin to countries that open their markets to EU companies through the WTO Government Procurement Agreement, free trade agreements or customs unions.

## Where the disagreement lies

The Competitiveness Council of 24 September only received [an information update](https://agenceurope.eu/en/bulletin/article/13937/11/eu27-ministers-invited-to-debate-semiconductors-and-mergers-at-24-september-competitiveness-council), although the Irish Presidency had hoped for a political agreement by that date. In the exchange, positions fell into two camps ([Borderlex, 24.9.2026](https://borderlex.net/2026/09/24/eu-ministers-still-split-on-scope-of-made-in-europe-preference/)):

- **Strict preference.** France argues that production must take place inside the EU, and Hungary opposes extending the preference to third countries.
- **Openness to trusted partners.** A broader group, including Belgium, Czechia, Denmark, Finland, Germany, Italy, Luxembourg, Poland and Spain, supports including partners with trade agreements and reciprocity. The case of the United Kingdom, European but outside the Union, is at the heart of the debate.

Other issues remain open: the weight of sustainability criteria in tenders, the definition of ‘low-carbon’ (Poland fears it could favour particular technologies) and the treatment of vehicles.

Spain's proposal seeks to bridge the gap. Industry minister Jordi Hereu put forward [a three-tier structure](https://www.infobae.com/america/agencias/2026/09/24/espana-propone-abrir-la-preferencia-europea-a-terceros-paises-en-algunos-sectores/) (in Spanish): a core made up of the EU-27; a second tier with the European Economic Area and reliable partners offering effective reciprocity; and a third with countries that have trade or customs agreements with the EU. Inclusion would be decided sector by sector. In the minister's words, the EU is the core of reindustrialisation, but the approach should be [‘pragmatic’ and ‘more granular’](https://www.pressdigital.es/articulo/economia/2026-09-24/6026524-espana-pide-pragmatismo-preferencia-europea-no-deje-fuera-industrias-socios-terceros-fiables) (in Spanish).

## The arguments on each side

The case for a strict preference is about effectiveness. If the perimeter is very wide, public money will keep financing capacity outside the Union and the act will not change investment decisions. France frames it through the British case: the UK chose to leave the Union and should not regain single-market advantages through this back door.

The opposing case is about cost and value chains. [Bruegel](https://www.bruegel.org/policy-brief/flaws-european-unions-proposed-industrial-accelerator-act-and-how-fix-them) (García Bercero, McWilliams, Tagliapietra and others, May 2026) argues that origin rules raise the cost of the transition, invite WTO challenges, and that the 20% GDP target lacks an economic rationale. It notes that more than three quarters of operational battery capacity in the EU belongs to South Korean companies and that two thirds of capacity under construction is Chinese. In its view, sustainability criteria such as those in the Net-Zero Industry Act would work better than origin. From the other end, parts of the European solar industry have criticised the proposal for not going far enough ([Carbon Brief](https://www.carbonbrief.org/qa-what-the-eus-new-industry-and-made-in-europe-rules-mean-for-climate-action)).

On paper, both sides want the same thing: more productive capacity in Europe. In practice, they disagree on how much they are willing to pay for it, in price and in time.

## What is at stake for Spain

Spain has interests on both sides. Its car industry, embedded in global supply chains, is among the most exposed to the requirements on electric vehicles and components, and to the conditions the act would place on foreign investment in batteries. At the same time, steel and cement are precisely the energy-intensive sectors the act seeks to protect through lead markets. My reading is that the tiered formula reflects this double exposure: it protects the industrial core without cutting supply chains that sustain jobs today.

The risk of a sector-by-sector approach is complexity. Different partner lists for each product would require contracting authorities, including municipalities and Spain's autonomous communities (its regional governments), to check origin against shifting criteria. If the act ends up hard to apply, the result may be less industry and more litigation.
