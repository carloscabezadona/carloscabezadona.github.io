Today, 29 September, the Council and the European Parliament hold their sixth trilogue on the regulation to prevent and combat online child sexual abuse, the file public debate knows as *Chat Control*. The question is no longer whether there will be an EU Centre or mandatory risk assessments, but something narrower and harder: what providers may do, and what authorities may order them to do, in private messages.

<div class="claves">
<h4>KEY MESSAGES</h4>
<ol>
<li>After four years, the disagreement has narrowed to detection in non-public content: the Council wants to keep broad voluntary detection; Parliament wants to limit it and place it under judicial or independent administrative oversight.</li>
<li>The interim regime allowing voluntary detection lapsed on 3 April 2026 and was only restored on 31 July. It now applies until 3 April 2028, which buys time but also removes urgency.</li>
<li>End-to-end encryption is the most visible limit: the interim regime expressly excludes it, and Parliament's mandate keeps it outside any detection order.</li>
</ol>

## Where the negotiation stands

The Commission tabled the [proposed regulation (COM(2022) 209)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:52022PC0209) in May 2022. Its centrepiece was the "detection order": a national authority could require a messaging or hosting service to search for known child sexual abuse material (CSAM), new material and the solicitation of children (grooming). It also creates an EU Centre on Child Sexual Abuse to manage indicator databases and receive reports.

Parliament adopted its mandate in [November 2023](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/news/en/press-room/20231110IPR10118/child-sexual-abuse-online-effective-measures-no-mass-surveillance), with the Spanish MEP Javier Zarzalejos (EPP) as rapporteur: detection orders only as a last resort, time-limited, targeted at users or groups under reasonable suspicion, and with end-to-end encryption excluded. The Council needed several presidencies to settle its position, reached on [26 November 2025](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2025/11/26/child-sexual-abuse-council-reaches-position-on-law-protecting-children-from-online-abuse/), which took a different route: no mandatory detection orders, voluntary detection made permanent, and services classified into three risk levels ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/my-europe/2025/11/27/eu-countries-reach-long-awaited-deal-on-online-child-abuse-detection); [EU Perspectives](https://euperspectives.eu/2025/11/eu-countries-agree-position-on-chat-contr/)).

Trilogues began on 9 December 2025. The fifth, on 29 June 2026, ended without agreement ([European Parliament, Legislative Train](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/legislative-train/spotlight-JD22/file-combating-child-sexual-abuse-online)). According to [Agence Europe](https://agenceurope.eu/en/bulletin/article/13943/15/csam-regulation-faced-with-deadlock-over-detection-irish-presidency-of-eu-council-seeks-to-refine-approach-between-public-and-private-content), the Irish Presidency has proposed separating public from private content. On the former there is a provisional technical agreement: detection orders limited to known material, and the EU Centre able to run its own searches. The latter remains blocked.

| | Council (mandate, Nov. 2025) | Parliament (mandate, Nov. 2023, and trilogue position) |
|---|---|---|
| Detection orders | Removed | Last resort, with reasonable suspicion and judicial authorisation |
| Voluntary detection in private messages | Permanent; known and new material and grooming | Limited to known material, under oversight of independent authorities |
| EU Centre | Yes | Yes |

## The spring precedent

Since 2021, [Regulation (EU) 2021/1232](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2021/1232/oj) has allowed messaging and email services, by way of derogation from the ePrivacy Directive, to detect such material voluntarily. In [December 2025](https://oeil.europarl.europa.eu/oeil/en/document-summary?id=1881350) the Commission proposed extending it to 2028. On 11 March Parliament backed a shorter extension, to August 2027 and with strict conditions ([458 votes to 103, with 63 abstentions](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/news/en/press-room/20260306IPR37531/child-sexual-abuse-online-support-for-extending-rules-until-august-2027)). Talks with the Council failed, and on 26 March the plenary rejected the extension ([228 in favour, 311 against, 92 abstentions](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/news/en/press-room/20260325IPR39207/child-sexual-abuse-online-voluntary-detection-measures-will-not-be-extended)). The derogation lapsed on 3 April.

The gap lasted almost four months. [Regulation (EU) 2026/1881](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1881/oj/eng) of 24 July, published on the 28th and in force since 31 July, restores the derogation until 3 April 2028 and expressly excludes end-to-end encrypted communications ([eucrim](https://eucrim.eu/news/interim-rules-on-voluntary-csam-detection-reinstated-until-2028/)). For reference, in late 2020, when the ePrivacy Directive began to apply to messaging with no derogation, reports received by the US National Center for Missing and Exploited Children (NCMEC) fell by 58% within 18 weeks ([Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/risk-and-compliance/an-uncertain-path-forward-the-eprivacy-derogation-and-child-safety-detection-102mopa)).

## The arguments on each side

The Council's position is, above all, about operational continuity. According to [Agence Europe](https://agenceurope.eu/en/bulletin/article/13943/15/csam-regulation-faced-with-deadlock-over-detection-irish-presidency-of-eu-council-seeks-to-refine-approach-between-public-and-private-content), member states want voluntary detection covering known material, new material and grooming in order to maintain current reporting levels. Narrowing it would reduce detection precisely where a victim can still be identified and protected: new material and grooming.

Parliament's position is about proportionality. Automated scanning of the private communications of millions of people without prior suspicion sits uneasily with Articles 7 and 8 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights, and the Court of Justice's case law on data retention has been demanding towards general and indiscriminate measures. There is also technical doubt: in March more than 800 scientists and researchers signed a letter questioning the reliability of these tools at scale, especially for new material, because of false positives ([Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/03/13/eu-parliament-extends-csam-rules/)).

My reading is that the technical agreement on public content shows where the negotiation could close: voluntary detection limited mainly to known material, with more oversight than today and the EU Centre as a control point. On paper, that protects the bulk of current reports. In practice, it would leave the hardest part, new material and grooming, in a grey zone to be settled in a later review.

## The Spanish angle

Spain comes to this debate with two pieces of its own. The first is Parliament's rapporteur, Javier Zarzalejos, who must defend before the Council a mandate adopted almost three years ago. The second is the [draft organic law on the protection of minors in digital environments](https://www.congreso.es/public_oficiales/L15/CONG/BOCG/A/BOCG-15-A-52-1.PDF) (in Spanish), which returns today to the rapporteurs' working group (*ponencia*) of the Justice Committee of the Congress of Deputies, the lower house of the Spanish parliament ([Infobae / Europa Press](https://www.infobae.com/america/agencias/2026/09/26/la-ley-que-prohibiria-las-redes-sociales-a-menores-de-16-anos-avanza-esta-semana-en-el-congreso/), in Spanish). The two texts do not overlap: the Spanish bill regulates minors' access to platforms; the EU regulation, the detection and removal of illegal material. But they share the same tension between child protection and privacy.

Criminal law runs on a separate track. In [June 2026](https://eurochild.org/news/eclag-statement-on-trilogue-agreement-on-the-recast-directive-on-combating-the-sexual-abuse-and-sexual-exploitation-of-children-and-child-sexual-abuse-material/) the Council and Parliament reached a provisional agreement on the recast directive on child sexual abuse, which covers AI-generated material and introduces minimum limitation periods. Transposing it will require amendments to national criminal codes, Spain's included.

The interim regime ensures that the spring gap will not recur before 2028, but it also takes pressure off the negotiators. Whether the permanent regulation arrives before then will depend on the Council accepting narrower voluntary detection, and on Parliament accepting that such detection continues to exist.
