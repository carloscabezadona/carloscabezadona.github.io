Almost €2.5 trillion a year in public works, supplies and services — 15% of the EU's GDP — is still governed by three 2014 directives that each member state transposed in its own way. On 9 September the Commission proposed ending that fragmentation: replacing them with a single regulation, directly applicable across the Twenty-Seven, which in Spain would strip most of its remaining function from the Public Sector Contracts Act.

## What changes

The proposal — formally COM(2026) 590, tabled under Article 114 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the EU — repeals Directives 2014/23/EU (concessions), 2014/24/EU (public contracts) and 2014/25/EU (the water, energy, transport and postal-services utilities), which until now each state had to transpose into its own legal order. Spain did so through Law 9/2017 on Public Sector Contracts (the LCSP). A regulation, by contrast, is directly applicable: it needs no national implementing law and leaves far less room for the variations that currently exist between Madrid, Paris or Berlin.

On the practical side, the reform simplifies award procedures, generalises the possibility of negotiating with bidders — until now reserved for specific cases — and makes the best price-quality ratio the general award criterion, with a mandatory minimum quality weighting of 30% (50% for labour-intensive contracts). It also creates a single digital procurement market, built on a "once-only" principle that stops companies having to resubmit the same documents to every contracting authority, plus data spaces to monitor competition and risk in tenders. The Commission puts the annual savings at €649 million: €80 million for contracting authorities, €570 million for economic operators, and a further €220 million from the digital platform itself.

## European preference

The most political element of the proposal is the horizontal "European preference" framework, designed to link public procurement to strategic autonomy and reduced dependence on third countries. It does not require European-preference clauses in every contract, but it clarifies the legal basis for contracting authorities to use them where there are economic-security risks or supply-chain vulnerabilities, and it gives the Commission the power to make them mandatory in specific sectors through delegated acts. The text draws on recent Court of Justice case law — *Kolin İnşaat* (C-652/22) and *CRRC Qingdao Sifang* (C-266/22) — on the treatment of non-EU bidders, turning into an explicit tool something that until now depended on case-by-case interpretation.

## The arguments for and against

The Commission's case rests on single-market coherence: with three directives transposed differently across twenty-seven legal systems, companies bidding in more than one member state face different rules, forms and deadlines for the same type of contract. A regulation removes that divergence in one stroke and, Brussels argues, frees up resources currently spent managing administrative complexity rather than building, supplying or innovating. Commissioner for Prosperity and Industrial Strategy Stéphane Séjourné summed it up in one line: it is "no longer just the price that counts."

The core objection has already come from legal analysis at European law firms such as Noerr: turning what was a directive into a regulation is not a minor technical change, because it removes the leeway each state had to fit the procedure to its own administrative tradition — in Germany, for instance, it would require repealing the national procurement regulation (the *Vergabeverordnung*) outright. The same analysis questions whether the new "open procedure with negotiation," which requires negotiating with every suitable, non-excluded bidder, will actually prove slower than the rules it replaces, and warns that the defence sector is left out of the reform despite sharing many of the same problems, leaving civilian and defence procurement uncoordinated.

## The case of Spain

For Spain, the shift from directive to regulation has a very concrete translation: much of the Public Sector Contracts Act, the law that has structured Spanish public procurement since 2017, loses its purpose wherever the regulation legislates directly and exhaustively. It will not disappear — it will still be needed for what remains unharmonised, for internal administrative organisation and for the sanctioning regime — but its scope for independent development shrinks by exactly as much as every other member state's. The Independent Office for the Regulation and Supervision of Procurement (OIReScon), created by the 2017 law precisely to oversee its application, will have to adapt its supervisory role to a text Madrid no longer decides.
