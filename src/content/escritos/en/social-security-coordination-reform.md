On 28 September the Council adopted the revision of the rules that coordinate national social security systems, a proposal the Commission tabled in December 2016. It concerns some [16 million Europeans](https://employment-social-affairs.ec.europa.eu/news/commission-welcomes-important-step-modernise-eu-social-security-coordination-rules-2026-04-30_en) who live or work in another Member State. The relevant question is whether the final text solves the problems that justified the reform or whether, after almost a decade, it has simply closed the file.

<h4>KEY MESSAGES</h4>
<ol>
<li>Jobseekers moving to another country will keep their unemployment benefit for six months instead of three, and cross-border workers will be paid by the country where they worked after 22 weeks.</li>
<li>Postings will require prior notification, except for business trips and activities of up to three days, with no exception for construction.</li>
<li>The weak point is enforcement: four Member States voted against for lack of guarantees on activation and data exchange, and the Commission has committed to fill the gap later.</li>

## What has changed

Social security is a national competence and is not harmonised. What the EU regulates is coordination: which Member State is competent and how contribution periods completed in different countries are added up, so that nobody loses cover by moving. That is the role of [Regulation (EC) No 883/2004](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2004/883/oj) and its implementing Regulation 987/2009, applicable since 2010.

The reform, based on Article 48 TFEU, covers five areas ([Council, 29.4.2026](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026/04/29/social-security-coordination-eu-member-states-representatives-confirm-provisional-agreement/)):

| Area | What changes |
|---|---|
| Unemployment | Benefits can be exported for six months, extendable to the end of entitlement. After 22 uninterrupted weeks of activity in another Member State, the country of last employment pays, not the country of residence. |
| Long-term care | A common definition and a list of coordinated benefits, to be evaluated by the Commission three years after application. |
| Economically inactive persons | The text refers to recent Court of Justice case law on access to social benefits by mobile citizens without economic activity. |
| Family benefits | Distinguishes benefits replacing income lost through child-raising from all others, so as not to penalise reduced working hours. |
| Postings and multi-state work | Three months of prior affiliation in the home country, a two-month break after 24 months of posting, mandatory prior notification and criteria for locating an employer's registered office. |

The road has been long. Several chapters were provisionally agreed in 2019, but the main issues stayed open for more than six years and took over twenty trilogues, according to the [European Parliamentary Research Service](https://eprs.europarl.europa.eu/contents/publications/EPRS/2026/06/EPRS_ATA(2026)789364.html). The provisional agreement came on 22 April 2026 and Parliament approved it at first reading on [7 July](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-11270-2026-INIT/en/pdf), with Gabriele Bischoff (S&D) as rapporteur. The [Council adopted it on 28 September](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026/09/28/social-security-coordination-council-gives-final-green-light-to-updated-rules/), with Denmark, Luxembourg, the Netherlands and Poland voting against and Hungary and Austria abstaining ([Council note 12261/26](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-12261-2026-INIT/en/pdf)).

## The case for

The argument in favour is legal certainty. The 2004 rules did not properly anticipate situations that are now common: long-term care provided to someone living in another country, workers insured in two Member States at once, or companies whose real seat is hard to pin down. Explicit definitions and criteria narrow the room for divergent interpretation by national administrations.

The reform also enjoys unusually broad backing. The European Trade Union Confederation urged Parliament to adopt it as [«a fair and acceptable compromise package»](https://www.etuc.org/en/circular/etuc-support-revision-social-security-coordination-regulations-plenary-vote-7-july), and the [European social partners](https://www.businesseurope.eu/publications/european-social-partners-call-on-the-council-and-parliament-to-endorse-the-provisional-agreement-on-the-revision-of-the-eu-regulations-on-social-security-coordination/), employers included, jointly called on the Council and Parliament to endorse the deal. The exemption for business trips and short activities answers a long-standing business complaint: having to request an A1 certificate, the document showing which system a posted worker is insured in, for a one-day meeting.

## The case against

The most serious objections come from the Member States that voted against, and they focus on two points.

**Controlling exported benefits.** In their view, extending the export of unemployment benefits from three to six months is not matched by binding tools to check that the jobseeker is actually looking for work in the host country. Luxembourg, where cross-border workers make up almost 47% of the workforce, regrets in its [statement](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-12261-2026-ADD-2/en/pdf) that the text does not mandate automated bulk data exchange on return to work or income. The Netherlands considers the unemployment chapter «not balanced». Belgium voted in favour, but made its support conditional on further Commission commitments.

**Prior notification and construction.** Luxembourg also criticises the new obligation on companies and the stricter sector-specific regime for construction, with sanctions it considers insufficiently defined. The text justifies the different treatment on the grounds that construction accounts for a high share of A1 certificates, of detected fraud and of workplace accidents. According to the [Commission's latest report on A1 documents](https://employment-social-affairs.ec.europa.eu/document/download/4595e14c-1f05-400d-bb70-c39a6949d1dc_en?filename=PD+A1+report+-+Reference+year+2023+.pdf), around 16% of posting A1s in 2023 related to construction, rising to 34% when Germany is excluded.

On paper, an answer to the first objection already exists: in a [statement for the minutes](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-12261-2026-ADD-1/en/pdf), the Commission commits to strengthening data exchange through the EESSI electronic system and to proposing improvements in the forthcoming Fair Labour Mobility Package. In practice, this is a political commitment, not a legal obligation.

## What it means for Spain

Spain is one of the main issuers of A1 certificates: more than 200,000 in 2023, behind Germany, Poland and Italy, according to the same report. Spanish construction, industrial assembly and service companies operating in other Member States will have to adapt to prior notification without the three-day exemption, as well as to the three-month prior affiliation rule for staff recruited to be posted. Spain was neither among the Member States voting against nor among those abstaining.

For workers, the most visible change concerns unemployment benefits. Jobseekers will be able to keep their benefit from Spain's public employment service (SEPE) for up to six months without relying on an extension. Cross-border workers living in Spain and working in, for example, France or Portugal will be paid by the country of last employment after 22 weeks of activity there, shifting the cost to the Member State where contributions were paid. My reading is that the budgetary effect will be modest, but the administrative burden on social security bodies will depend on the quality of electronic data exchange.

The reform belatedly fills many of the gaps left in 2004. Whether it also strengthens trust between administrations will depend on the data exchange that is today a commitment becoming an obligation before the new rules start to apply.
