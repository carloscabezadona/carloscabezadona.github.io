On 17 September the European Commission presents the EU Kids Act, its first attempt at a common minimum age across the Union for opening an account on social media, online games and AI chatbots. The draft that has circulated since the weekend confirms the overall design — age tiers, mandatory verification, "safe by design" rules — but leaves open the figure that divides member states the most: 13, 15 or 16?

## What the draft proposes

According to leaked versions reported by [The Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/ireland/2026/09/14/eu-to-bring-in-minimum-age-limit-of-15-to-set-up-social-media-accounts/) and [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/my-europe/2026/09/15/leak-eu-commission-to-pitch-social-media-restrictions-for-under-15s), the EU Kids Act splits minors' access into tiers: no access under 3; ages 3-13, only "child-friendly" services under full parental control; ages 13-15, "introductory" accounts with limited features and supervision; from 15 (or whatever figure is ultimately set), an autonomous account. The text, framed as a directly applicable regulation according to [ppc.land](https://ppc.land/eu-kids-act-draft-sets-15-as-age-for-unsupervised-social-media-accounts/), covers social media, video-sharing platforms, online games and AI chatbots or "companions", excluding educational services and public administrations.

Platforms would have to verify age at account creation, using an EU-wide app or equivalent national solutions meeting privacy and security standards, and apply "safe by design" principles: no infinite scroll, no notifications or reward mechanisms engineered to build habit, adjustable recommender systems, private-by-default profiles, and blocking contact from strangers. The largest services would have to submit audited compliance plans to the Commission and pay a supervisory fee, according to reporting by [MLex](https://www.mlex.com/mlex/articles/2525368/platforms-face-fees-reporting-duties-under-draft-eu-kids-act). It will be presented by President Ursula von der Leyen and Executive Vice-President Henna Virkkunen.

## The case for, and the case that does not convince

The case for is one of single-market coherence. Seventeen member states are already drafting or negotiating their own minimum-age rules; without a common EU standard, platforms would face a patchwork of incompatible obligations. The "safe by design" approach — curbing infinite scroll or streak-based rewards — is also more proportionate than an outright ban, since it targets specific mechanisms of engagement rather than access itself.

The substantive objection was set out by the French Constitutional Council, which on 14 August [struck down](https://www.france24.com/en/france/20260814-france-s-constitutional-authority-strikes-down-social-media-ban-for-under-15s) France's ban on social media for under-15s as disproportionate: it treated a risky network the same as an educational platform, failed to distinguish each service's actual risk, and did not safeguard the privacy of those required to prove their age. An EU rule built on the same design could inherit these objections at greater scale. There is also a practical doubt: reliably verifying the age of hundreds of millions of users requires a system that works, and the one the Commission has already deployed does not yet.

## Spain and the member-state coalition

Spain initially backed, together with the European Parliament, a minimum age of 16; the Commission's technical panel proposed 13; France holds out for 15. That lack of internal agreement is why the draft reaches its presentation without a settled figure, according to [wwwhatsnew](https://wwwhatsnew.com/2026/09/15/ue-kids-act-jueves-edad-limite-sin-fijar-safe-design-2026/) (in Spanish). Spain and the Netherlands did succeed in widening the scope beyond social media, to include video games and AI chatbots.

Spain's own government is separately processing a bill — an organic law on the protection of minors in digital environments — that would raise the minimum age for social media accounts to 16. It remains [in parliamentary proceedings with no voting date set](https://www.lamoncloa.gob.es/presidente/actividades/Paginas/2026/090926-sanchez-sesion-control-.aspx?qfr=1), and Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez urged Congress on 9 September to pass it "once and for all", after Spain recorded its worst-ever PISA results in reading, mathematics and science (451, 457 and 477 points, against OECD averages of 461, 463 and 482) — a decline the government links, though the OECD itself does not establish that causal claim, to screen use ([Infobae](https://www.infobae.com/america/agencias/2026/09/08/espana-vuelve-a-suspender-en-pisa-obtiene-en-2025-sus-peores-resultados-historicos-en-lectura-matematicas-y-ciencias/), in Spanish). France, despite having its own law struck down on constitutional grounds, likewise backs an EU-level rule: a Union regulation grounded in internal-market harmonisation can overcome objections a single member state cannot.

## The weak link: verifying age

The whole edifice rests on age verification working without exposing unnecessary data. The Commission unveiled its zero-knowledge-proof-based verification app in April 2026, designed to prove a user is above an age threshold without revealing their identity. Security researcher Paul Moore bypassed it in under two minutes by manipulating an editable configuration file that allowed a PIN reset and disabled the biometric check ([Proton](https://proton.me/blog/eu-age-verification-app-hacked)). After a hardened version was released in July, Moore bypassed it again, this time with two browser extensions he said he built in minutes with AI assistance ([Cybernews](https://cybernews.com/security/eu-age-verification-app-bypassed-again/)). The problem is not the cryptographic design but its implementation — and it is precisely this app, or its equivalents, that the EU Kids Act would make mandatory.
