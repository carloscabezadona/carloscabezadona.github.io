The EU wants more electricity grids, built faster, and there is little disagreement on that. There is, and it is substantial, on who decides which grid is needed and with which tools. On 10 September the European Parliament's Committee on Industry, Research and Energy (ITRE) set its position on the revision of the Trans-European Networks for Energy (TEN-E) Regulation, and that position departs from the one the Council adopted in June.

## What is being negotiated

On 10 December 2025 the Commission presented the [European grids package](https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/eu-plans-upgrade-energy-infrastructure-lower-bills-and-boost-independence-2025-12-10_en). Its logic is to make better use of existing infrastructure, speed up permitting and share the costs of cross-border projects more fairly. It contains two legislative proposals: to [repeal and replace the TEN-E](https://energy.ec.europa.eu/topics/infrastructure/european-grids_en) (today [Regulation (EU) 2022/869](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/869/oj)) and a directive that accelerates permit-granting by amending the renewables, electricity and gas and hydrogen directives. The Commission puts the investment needed in grids by 2030 at EUR 584 billion.

Two ideas in the original text now divide the institutions. The first is a “central scenario”, built at European level, on which electricity, hydrogen and gas networks are planned. The second is that “non-wire” alternatives (flexibility, storage, technologies that raise the capacity of existing lines) should be considered before new infrastructure.

## The Council's position

The Council set its general approach on [26 June 2026](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026/06/26/european-grids-package-council-backs-modernised-energy-network-for-decarbonisation/), under the Cypriot presidency. It accepted a central scenario developed by the Commission with input from governments and stakeholders, but with more control for Member States. According to [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/my-europe/2026/06/26/eu-energy-ministers-secure-greater-role-in-blocs-future-power-grid-after-rows-with-brussel), countries verify the data and assumptions, and keep some room over where congestion income goes (10 % of unused revenue would go to interconnectors from January 2028, rising to 25 % in 2031).

Two points from analysts. For [CATF](https://www.catf.us/2026/07/the-next-chapter-for-europes-electricity-grids-what-the-councils-general-approach-gets-right-and-what-it-still-needs/), moving the scenario from a delegated act to an implementing act gives Member States a qualified-majority veto and “re-nationalises” part of the planning, and digital permitting portals become optional. And according to [CurrENT](https://www.currenteurope.eu/news/meps-support-the-prioritisation-of-non-wire-solutions-in-todays-ten-e-vote/), the Council removed the priority for non-wire solutions in both the TEN-E and the permitting directive.

## The Parliament's position

On 10 September ITRE adopted the TEN-E text by [56 votes to 10, with 9 abstentions](https://agenceurope.eu/en/bulletin/article/13935/9/networks-package-european-parliament-industry-committee-backs-centralised-but-transparent-approach-on-ten-e-regulation). According to the same outlet, the committee backs a bigger role for the European Commission in the strategic framework, with the Agency for the Cooperation of Energy Regulators (ACER) providing independent scrutiny, as opposed to the Council's preference for regional operators. It also voted, 61 to 6 with 8 abstentions, to open negotiations with the Council, which the plenary must confirm. The report on the permitting directive had been adopted in ITRE on 2 July. According to [Agence Europe](https://agenceurope.eu/en/bulletin/article/13910/35/grids-package-political-negotiations-between-european-parliament-and-eu-council-on-permitting-directive-to-be-held-in-october), the political phase of the trilogue (the three-way negotiation between Parliament, Council and Commission) is expected in October, with the aim of finishing before the end of 2026.

| | Council (26.06.2026) | Parliament (ITRE, 10.09.2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Planning | More weight for Member States on data and assumptions; central scenario | Bigger role for the Commission, with ACER scrutiny |
| Non-wire solutions | No express priority | Priority consideration and a broad definition of grid technologies |

## The arguments

The case for Parliament's approach is coherence: a grid that works as a European system needs common planning and an independent referee, so that each Member State does not simply defend its own section. Prioritising non-wire solutions can also be faster and cheaper than opening a new corridor.

The Council's case is about legitimacy and accountability. Member States pay for, authorise and answer to their citizens for the lines that cross their territory, and do not want a European scenario to condition investment decisions that are national. A practical argument can be added: a text that requires alternatives to be considered first may lengthen the procedures it is meant to shorten.

My reading is that both have a point, and that the middle ground lies in procedure: who approves the scenario and with what margin of veto, and whether the priority for non-wire solutions is an analytical criterion or a prior hurdle.

## The Spanish angle

The Iberian Peninsula is among the [eight “energy highways”](https://energy.ec.europa.eu/topics/infrastructure/european-grids_en), with the Pyrenean crossings as the bottleneck. The EU interconnection target is [15 % by 2030](https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/eu-plans-upgrade-energy-infrastructure-lower-bills-and-boost-independence-2025-12-10_en). The Bay of Biscay interconnector, an EU project of common interest, is moving forward: according to [Energynews](https://www.energynews.es/interconexion-electrica-francia-golfo-vizcaya-cable-submarino/) (in Spanish), it is 60 % complete, laying of the 276 km subsea section has begun, and it is expected to raise exchange capacity from 2,800 to 5,000 MW in 2028, after three stoppages on the land sections. My reading is that this project is a good test bed: its stoppages have come from effects reported by the affected municipalities, that is, from permitting and local acceptance, more than from European planning.

Transmission operators are also asking legislators to speed up procedures. On 15 September, [ENTSO-E warned](https://asena-consulting.es/noticias/los-operadores-electricos-alertan-mas-de-la-mitad-de-las-redes-que-europa-necesita-para-2030-sigue-sin-permisos/) (as reported in the Spanish press, in Spanish) that more than half of the transmission projects needed by 2030 still lack the required permits, and that storage and demand response will not replace the need to develop networks on time.

## Assessment

The package may well end in a reasonable compromise, because the two institutions share the diagnosis and the calendar is pressing. The risk lies elsewhere: that the negotiation spends its time on the division of powers and leaves for later what weighs most on the ground, namely permitting deadlines and local acceptance. Whether the reform really speeds up the grid will depend less on who approves the central scenario than on whether authorisation deadlines are met in each project.
