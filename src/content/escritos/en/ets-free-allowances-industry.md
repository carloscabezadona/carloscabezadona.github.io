On 16 September, the Twenty-Seven's ambassadors set the Council's position to expand by 121 million the free carbon allowances given to industrial sectors most exposed to carbon leakage. The decision builds on a Commission proposal from July and raises a deeper question: how far can shielding industry from the cost of CO2 still be squared with pushing it to decarbonise?

## What has changed

The EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS, launched in 2005) requires industrial installations to cover their CO2 emissions with allowances, but hands out a share for free to sectors most exposed to competitors in countries without comparable climate rules, so they are not driven to relocate production outside the EU. Free allocation is calculated through benchmarks: the emissions level of the cleanest 10% of installations in each sector.

On 11 May 2026 the Commission updated these benchmark values for 2026-2030: industry keeps, on average, free coverage of around 75% of its emissions, with an additional boost worth around €4 billion to incentivise electrification across 14 product benchmarks ([European Commission, IP/26/1044](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_1044)). That calculation, however, left sectors relying on the so-called heat and fuel benchmarks — generic fallback values used when there is not enough data for a product-specific one — worse covered.

To fix that, on 17 July the Commission tabled a separate proposal adjusting the maximum annual reduction rate applied to those two benchmarks, unlocking around 80 million additional allowances for 2026-2030, worth an estimated €6 billion, excluding oil and gas ([Council document ST-12075-2026](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-12075-2026-INIT/en/pdf); [European Commission, Representation in Malta, 17.07.2026](https://malta.representation.ec.europa.eu/news/commission-boosts-europes-competitiveness-decarbonisation-and-independence-electrification-action-2026-07-17_en)). That same day, the Commission also presented the broader review of the EU ETS for 2031-2040, which extends free allocation beyond 2030, slows its phase-out in CBAM-covered sectors until 2038, and sets up a €100 billion Industrial Decarbonisation Bank.

On 16 September, the Council (at ambassador level, Coreper) went further than the July proposal: it added another 33 million allowances, previously withheld for failing to meet the scheme's conditionality requirements, to the 80 million, for a total of 121 million ([Council of the EU, press release, 16.09.2026](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026/09/16/ets-council-agrees-targeted-measure-to-protect-energy-intensive-sectors-from-carbon-leakage/); [Coreper mandate, ST-13134-2026](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-13134-2026-INIT/en/pdf)). Ireland's Minister for Climate, Energy and the Environment, Darragh O'Brien, who holds the rotating presidency, called the agreement "important for the environment and for EU industry" and said negotiations with the European Parliament would start as soon as it sets its own position.

## The case for, and the case that does not convince

The institutional case is one of proportionality and competitiveness. Rising energy prices and the geopolitical context have pressured sectors such as steel, cement and chemicals, which compete against non-EU producers facing no equivalent CO2 cost. Handing out more free allowances, the Council argues, protects jobs while progress towards climate targets continues, in line with the Clean Industrial Deal and the June 2026 European Council conclusions.

The criticism does not deny the carbon-leakage risk, but questions the timing and direction of the change. A Bruegel survey of firms under the EU ETS found little evidence of actual carbon leakage and noted that around 60% of firms already received more free allowances than they needed to cover their emissions — meaning they were partly shielded from the real carbon price ([Bruegel, "The EU emissions trading system works but most industrial decarbonisation is still to come"](https://www.bruegel.org/analysis/eu-emissions-trading-system-works-most-industrial-decarbonisation-still-come)). The gradual reduction of free allocation, which had already started applying in January 2026, is precisely the mechanism that gives the EU ETS its force as an incentive to invest in decarbonisation. Expanding it now, even if narrowly targeted at two benchmarks, runs against that trajectory — just as eight in ten firms surveyed by Bruegel report already having a decarbonisation strategy under way.

## The Spanish case

Spain, like every other member state, applies the same common free-allocation rules. On 26 August, the Council of Ministers approved the final allocation for 2026-2030: 664 fixed industrial installations will receive an average of 38.1 million free allowances a year, worth around €3 billion annually ([MITECO, press release, 26.08.2026](https://www.miteco.gob.es/es/prensa/ultimas-noticias/2026/agosto/el-gobierno-aprueba-la-asignacion-final-gratuita-de-derechos-de-.html)). Compared with 2025, the allocation falls by 1.6%, so the free-coverage deficit for Spanish industry rises from 20.0% to 21.3%: each year, a slightly larger share of its emissions will need to be covered with allowances bought on the market.

That Spanish figure confirms the general downward trajectory of free allocation remains intact, even as the EU negotiates giving more room to sectors under the heat and fuel benchmarks. Since 2005, emissions covered by the EU ETS have fallen 62% in Spain, above the roughly 50% EU-wide average cited by the Commission, suggesting much of Spain's heavy industry has already made the hardest part of the adjustment.

## My reading

The Council's agreement is defensible as a targeted response to a technical asymmetry — the heat and fuel benchmarks were penalising specific sectors more than intended — rather than as an abandonment of the decarbonisation trajectory, which in Spain and across the EU keeps reducing free coverage year after year. My reading, however, is that the room gained in Brussels will only make environmental sense if it comes with the link, already planned for after 2031, between free allocation and verified investment in decarbonisation. Without that condition, 121 million extra allowances are simply more time bought.
