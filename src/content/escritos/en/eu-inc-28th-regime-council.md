The European Council asked for the "28th regime" for companies to be agreed before the end of 2026. At today's Competitiveness Council, on 24 September, the agenda provides only for an information point on it. The relevant question is not whether Member States support the idea, which they do, but which version of EU Inc. will survive the issues that technical negotiations have yet to settle.

## What the Commission proposes

On 18 March 2026 the Commission tabled its [proposal for a Regulation on the 28th regime (EU Inc.)](https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/eu-inc-making-business-easier-european-union-2026-03-18_en), COM(2026) 321. It follows the ordinary legislative procedure ([2026/0074(COD)](https://oeil.europarl.europa.eu/oeil/en/procedure-file?reference=2026/0074(COD))) and rests on Article 114 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the EU (TFEU), the internal market harmonisation clause. The starting diagnosis is fragmentation: 27 legal systems and more than 60 company forms, with incorporation taking weeks or even months.

The answer does not replace national companies. It sits alongside them as a "twenty-eighth" regime. According to the [Council Presidency note](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-8598-2026-INIT/en/pdf), its main elements are incorporation within 48 hours for less than €100, digital procedures throughout the company's life, once-only transmission of data to authorities, faster insolvency procedures and an EU employee stock options scheme with harmonised timing of taxation. The simplified winding-up procedure is reserved for innovative start-ups with fewer than 100 employees, turnover of up to €10 million and less than ten years of activity ([Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/transactions/eu-inc-the-insolvency-provisions-of-the-commissions-draft-28th-regime-proposal-102mq7y)).

## Where the negotiations stand

| Institution | State of play |
|---|---|
| European Council | [Conclusions of 19 March](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-8598-2026-INIT/en/pdf): agreement by the co-legislators by the end of 2026 |
| European Parliament | Legislative-initiative resolution of [20 January 2026](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/doceo/document/TA-10-2026-0002_EN.html) (492 in favour, 144 against, 28 abstentions). JURI rapporteur: René Repasi (S&D); plenary vote indicatively scheduled for 19 October |
| Council | Policy debate on [28 May](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-9847-2026-INIT/en/pdf); on 24 September, only [information from the Presidency](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-13104-2026-INIT/en/pdf) under any other business |

The note prepared for the May debate sums up the Council's position well. Delegations maintain general scrutiny reservations but have expressed "strong support" for the initiative. Still to be clarified are the legal basis, insolvency, taxation, the risk of forum shopping (choosing the most convenient Member State of registration), co-determination and the need for further safeguards.

## The case for

The argument is about the single market. A start-up growing across several Member States today faces 27 different frameworks for incorporation, share transfers and incentive schemes. A single, digital regime recognised throughout the Union removes fixed costs that weigh more heavily the smaller the company. Parliament, moreover, asked for this regime before the Commission proposed it, and by a comfortable majority.

## The objections

The first is legal. Article 114(2) TFEU excludes fiscal provisions from qualified majority voting; they require unanimity under Article 115. A stock options scheme with harmonised tax timing sits awkwardly on an internal market legal basis, and several Member States can be expected to challenge it. On paper, the problem is solved by splitting off the tax element. In practice, that would strip EU Inc. of one of its most visible advantages for attracting talent.

The second is social. The proposal leaves board-level worker participation to the law of the Member State of the registered office. European trade unions and the [Workers' Group of the European Economic and Social Committee](https://www.eesc.europa.eu/en/news-media/press-releases/28th-regime-why-are-alarm-bells-ringing) (EESC) warn that this allows companies to register where co-determination is weakest and operate elsewhere, with a risk of letterbox companies. The Commission replies that national labour and social law is unaffected. Both can be true at once: employment contracts are still generally governed by the law of the place where the work is carried out ([Regulation (EC) No 593/2008, Art. 8](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2008/593/oj)), but whether workers sit on the board does depend on the registered office.

The third concerns design. [Fiona Scott Morton and Reinhilde Veugelers (Bruegel)](https://www.bruegel.org/analysis/how-secure-benefits-eu-wide-incorporation-regime) argue that a regime open to any company dilutes the goal of serving innovative scale-ups, and forces compromises that do not address their specific barriers.

## The Spanish case

Spain comes to the table with much of its national homework done. [Law 18/2022 on business creation and growth](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-2022-15818) (in Spanish), published in the BOE, Spain's official gazette, cut the minimum capital of a limited company (*sociedad limitada*) to €1 and allows incorporation within 24 hours using standard articles. [Law 28/2022 on start-ups](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-2022-21739) (in Spanish) raised to €50,000 a year the tax exemption for shares these companies grant to their employees. For a Spanish founder, then, EU Inc. adds less in speed or cost at home than in the ability to enter France, Germany or Italy with the same legal form and without repeating formalities.

That cuts two ways. The positive reading: the start-up scenes in Madrid, Barcelona or Valencia gain access to a 27-country market without restructuring costs. The point to watch: if the EU stock options scheme ends up less generous than Spain's, or falls away on tax grounds, the relative advantage of the 2022 law remains and EU Inc. loses appeal in Spain. The co-determination debate, by contrast, plays out mainly in Member States with mandatory board-level employee representation, such as Germany or Sweden ([Corporate Finance Lab](https://corporatefinancelab.org/2026/04/10/the-eu-inc-proposal-how-far-does-it-really-go/)).

## Assessment

My reading is that the end-2026 deadline is achievable for a slimmed-down text, not for the full package. If taxation and insolvency are taken out of the regulation to secure a qualified majority, EU Inc. will mainly be a digital, portable company form. That is useful, but it falls short of the scale-up instrument called for in the [Letta](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/media/ny3j24sm/much-more-than-a-market-report-by-enrico-letta.pdf) and [Draghi](https://commission.europa.eu/topics/eu-competitiveness/draghi-report_en) reports in 2024.
