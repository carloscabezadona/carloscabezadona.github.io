On 19 November 2025 the European Commission proposed simplifying the regulation that has defined data protection in Europe since 2018. Ten months on, with the Council and Parliament still each negotiating their own position, a document leaked this week shows how far that simplification could go, and why some of those watching it are calling it a rollback.

## What the Commission is proposing

The Digital Omnibus (COM(2025) 837 final) is part of the simplification agenda through which the Commission wants to cut businesses' administrative costs by 25%, and SMEs' by 35%, before the end of the mandate. The package touches the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), the ePrivacy Directive, the Data Governance Act and the Platform-to-Business (P2B) Regulation, among other rules ([European Commission, 19.11.2025](https://spain.representation.ec.europa.eu/noticias-eventos/noticias-0/la-simplificacion-de-normas-digitales-y-las-nuevas-carteras-digitales-permitiran-ahorrar-e-impulsar-2025-11-19_es)).

Three changes are at the centre of the debate. First, the definition of "personal data": the proposal introduces a subjective test under which information counts as personal for a given entity only if that entity, using means reasonably available to it, can identify the person concerned. Second, cookie consent moves out of the GDPR into a revised ePrivacy Regulation, with a single click valid for up to six months. Third, and most contested, a new article (numbered 88c in the Commission's proposal, 88a in the Council text) recognises processing personal data to develop and operate AI systems as a "legitimate interest", without requiring the data subject's consent as a starting point.

This is a separate file from the "Digital Omnibus on AI", which delayed obligations under the AI Act and which Parliament and Council already closed last June. The one touching the GDPR is still being negotiated in both institutions.

## The case for, and the case that does not convince

The Commission presents the package as the first step of a broader digital simplification programme, needed so European businesses are not left at a disadvantage against rivals facing fewer regulatory demands. Executive Vice-President Henna Virkkunen has framed cutting red tape as a condition for closing the innovation gap with the United States and China. Business associations such as DigitalEurope and CCIA Europe have welcomed the package as a necessary, if insufficient, step: they want the GDPR review for AI developers to go further ([DigitalEurope](https://www.digitaleurope.org/resources/digital-omnibus-a-first-step-and-what-must-come-next-now/); [CCIA Europe](https://ccianet.org/news/2025/11/digital-omnibus-simplification-of-eu-tech-rules-requires-bolder-action/)).

The criticism, first voiced by 127 civil society organisations, trade unions and public-interest groups in an open letter in November 2025, and later backed by the European Data Protection Board (EDPB, which brings together the data protection authorities of all 27 member states, including Spain's AEPD) and the European Data Protection Supervisor, is that the package goes beyond technical simplification and touches core GDPR principles without a full impact assessment on fundamental rights ([EDPB-EDPS Joint Opinion 2/2026, 11.02.2026](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/our-work-tools/our-documents/edpbedps-joint-opinion/edpb-edps-joint-opinion-22026-proposal_en)). Their most-cited example: personalised advertising without consent, unlawful today, could become lawful if the processing is framed as training or running an AI system.

## What changed between November and September

The Commission's original proposal already included safeguards for that new AI article: an unconditional right to object to the processing, data-minimisation duties during model training and testing, stronger transparency requirements, and an explicit reference to the protection of children. The compromise text from the Irish Council Presidency (document 12535/26, dated 3 September 2026 and discussed in a working group on 11 September), published this week by the privacy organisation noyb alongside drafting suggestions from the German government, strips out several of those safeguards and routes the test through the balancing-of-interests clause in GDPR Article 6(1)(f) instead ([noyb, 21.09.2026](https://noyb.eu/sites/default/files/2026-09/Draft%20Compromise.pdf); [ppc.land analysis](https://ppc.land/eu-council-draft-drops-unconditional-opt-out-from-gdpr-ai-clause/)).

| | Commission proposal (11/2025) | Council compromise (09/2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Right to object | Unconditional | Subject to balancing of interests |
| Data minimisation for AI | Explicit obligation in the articles | Moved to recitals, non-binding |
| Protection of children | Explicit mention | Removed from the article |
| Legal basis | Legitimate interest with specific conditions | Legitimate interest by default ("may be regarded as") |

This is a working text among member states, not a settled Council position, let alone the final rule: the Council still has to agree its negotiating mandate, Parliament has to close its own (the rapporteurs, Estonia's Marina Kaljurand for LIBE and Finland's Aura Salla for ITRE, tabled their report on 22 June and are still working through more than 1,750 amendments), and both institutions then have to negotiate in trilogue.

## What is at stake

On paper, the Commission's goal is reasonable: a GDPR written in 2016, before generative AI models existed, creates real legal uncertainty today, and the EDPB itself accepts the need for clarification. In practice, the concrete shape that clarification takes decides whether the result is a more workable rule, or one that gives large AI platforms a default basis to use personal data without consent, leaving the data subject to object case by case. My reading is that the risk is not in simplifying the GDPR, but in doing so through an omnibus regulation negotiated on a tight timetable, without the impact assessment the EDPB itself said was missing.
