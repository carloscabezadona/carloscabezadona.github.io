For years, EU climate policy has been measured almost entirely in tonnes of CO₂ avoided. The State of the Union address of 16 September reorders the priorities: adaptation, that is, preparing for impacts that can no longer be avoided, moves to the front. The relevant question is whether the announcements will turn into obligations and resources, or simply add another layer of plans.

## What has been announced

In her [address of 16 September](https://luxembourg.representation.ec.europa.eu/actualites-et-evenements/actualites/2026-state-union-address-president-von-der-leyen-2026-09-16_en), the Commission President took stock of the summer: 660,000 hectares burnt, some 12 million tonnes of crops lost and more than 35,000 additional deaths during the heatwaves. She then announced four measures. The [letter of intent](https://commission.europa.eu/publications/state-union-2026-documents_en) to the Parliament and the Irish Council Presidency groups them under "Climate and resilience".

| Initiative | What has been said | How concrete |
|---|---|---|
| Integrated climate resilience framework | To be presented "next month"; will identify the 100 most vulnerable territories in Europe | High: public consultation closed, planned for Q4 2026 |
| European Heatwave Plan | Early warning, health preparedness, urban adaptation and cooling, support for the most vulnerable | Low: headlines only |
| European Water Initiative | Risk management in agriculture and the insurance gap | Low |
| Climate Insurance Alliance | Closing the coverage gap | Low: format to be defined |

The resilience framework is the most advanced piece. According to the [European Parliament's Legislative Train](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/legislative-train/theme-a-new-plan-for-europe-s-sustainable-prosperity-and-competitiveness/file-european-framework-for-climate-resilience-(1)), it would include a common climate reference scenario based on a 3°C global warming trajectory, a harmonised methodology for climate risk assessments by Member States, sectors and private actors, a "risk ownership" principle clarifying who is responsible for each risk in health, energy, transport or defence, and "climate resilience by design" in investment and policy. The evidence base is the first European Climate Risk Assessment, which identified 36 major risks, several already at critical levels.

On water, the announcement builds on the [European Water Resilience Strategy](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_25_1404) of 4 June 2025, which aims to improve water efficiency by at least 10% by 2030. On 21 September the Commission [launched](https://environment.ec.europa.eu/news/commission-launches-water-resilience-stakeholder-platform-2026-09-21_en) the strategy's stakeholder platform, with more than 50 experts, the European Water Academy and an investment accelerator for 20 pilot projects on natural water retention and efficiency.

## The case for

The strongest argument is fiscal. A [Bruegel policy brief of June 2026](https://www.bruegel.org/policy-brief/planning-rising-fiscal-costs-climate-disasters) puts direct damage from climate disasters in the EU at €822 billion between 1980 and 2024, a quarter of it in the last four years. Since most of these losses are uninsured, national budgets end up as insurers of last resort, with ad hoc relief after each disaster. The Commission President used that very phrase in her speech.

A common reference scenario also has a single-market logic. If each Member State plans on different climate assumptions, cross-border infrastructure (power grids, transport corridors, shared river basins) is designed to incompatible standards. The [European Scientific Advisory Board on Climate Change](https://climate-advisory-board.europa.eu/reports-and-publications/20260217_adaptation-report.pdf/@@download/file) asked for exactly this in February: mandatory, harmonised risk assessments across EU policies, with a common planning reference.

## The case against

The first objection concerns competences. Land-use planning, health and much of water management are national competences and, in decentralised states such as Spain, regional or local ones (Spain's autonomous communities hold broad powers in these areas). On paper, a European framework coordinates. In practice, identifying "vulnerable territories" from Brussels raises questions of subsidiarity and of who is expected to do what.

The second concerns effectiveness. The Parliament's tracker classifies the framework as non-legislative, although the Commission speaks of a package mixing different types of instruments. If recommendations and guidance dominate, the outcome may resemble the 2021 Adaptation Strategy, which carried no obligations of its own. The Commission presents the new framework as a [response](https://climate.ec.europa.eu/areas-action/adaptation-and-resilience-climate-change/european-climate-resilience-and-risk-management-integrated-framework_en) to requests from the Parliament and the Council and to a 2024 European Court of Auditors report, which suggests the previous approach was seen as insufficient.

The third is budgetary. None of the announcements comes with a figure. Funding will depend on the 2028-2034 Multiannual Financial Framework, still under negotiation in the Council.

## Climate insurance and the Spanish model

The insurance gap is where the debate could become concrete first. According to the [dashboard of EIOPA](https://www.eiopa.europa.eu/tools-and-data/dashboard-insurance-protection-gap-natural-catastrophes_en), the European Insurance and Occupational Pensions Authority, only around a quarter of losses from extreme events in Europe were insured between 1980 and 2024, with some countries below 5%.

Spain starts from an unusual position. The Consorcio de Compensación de Seguros, a public entity funded through a compulsory surcharge on private policies, covers extraordinary risks such as floods and storms. EIOPA cites it, together with France's CCR, as a [model of public-private partnership](https://www.eiopa.europa.eu/publications/bridging-insurance-protection-gaps-regulation-incentives-and-coordination_en). The 2024 Valencia floods showed both its strength and its limit: according to Bruegel, the Consorcio paid out around €4.8 billion, the largest compensation in its history, using roughly a third of its reserves. My reading is that the Spanish case supports both extending similar national schemes and creating a European backstop for events beyond the capacity of any single system.

Heat also has a clear Spanish dimension. The MoMo daily mortality monitoring system of the Carlos III Health Institute attributed 5,247 deaths to high temperatures between 15 May and 1 September, above the 4,789 of 2022 ([Climática](https://climatica.coop/verano-muertes-por-calor-espana-europa/), in Spanish, based on [Ministry of Health](https://www.sanidad.gob.es/areas/sanidadAmbiental/riesgosAmbientales/calorExtremo/momoAltasTemperaturas.htm) data, in Spanish). Spain already has a [national heat prevention plan](https://www.sanidad.gob.es/areas/sanidadAmbiental/riesgosAmbientales/calorExtremo/publicaciones/docs/planNacionalExcesoTemperaturas_2026.pdf) (in Spanish), so the added value of a European plan would lie in shared early warning, adapting hospitals and care homes, and funding, rather than in duplicating protocols.

## Assessment

The shift is real in the rhetoric and still open in substance. The Commission has identified the problem well: the costs of climate change are already being paid, but in a disorderly way and mostly with public money. Whether the new framework changes that will depend on it setting verifiable responsibilities for who manages each risk, and on the next EU budget backing it with resources.
