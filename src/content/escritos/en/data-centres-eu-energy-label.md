Data centres have become one of Europe's major new consumers of electricity, and the EU wants to triple their capacity within a few years. On 21 September the Commission proposed a common label, from A to G, for their energy and water use. The relevant question is whether an informative label is enough to steer that growth, or whether its real value will lie in how regulators choose to use it.

## What the Commission has proposed

The [recast Energy Efficiency Directive (EU) 2023/1791](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2023/1791/oj) (EED) required data centres to report on their performance. [Delegated Regulation (EU) 2024/1364](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg_del/2024/1364/oj) set out the indicators and created a European database. The 21 September proposal is the second step: using the same data, the European database will generate an annual label for every data centre with 500 kW or more of installed IT power ([European Commission](https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/making-data-centres-energy-efficient-thanks-new-eu-rating-system-2026-09-21_en)). The first will be issued on 15 August 2027.

The label has two scales. The energy scale is based on power usage effectiveness (PUE, the ratio between the facility's total consumption and that of its IT equipment): an A requires a PUE of 1.15 or lower, while G means above 1.9. The water scale is based on water usage effectiveness (WUE, litres per IT kWh): A up to 0.1 and G above 1.0. The label also shows energy sources, the capacity to offer flexibility to the grid and waste heat reuse ([Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/sustainability/from-reporting-to-rating-the-eus-next-steps-for-data-centres-102o345)).

The procedure matters. As a delegated act, the European Parliament and the Council have two months, extendable by two more, to object, but they cannot amend the text. In parallel, the Commission has opened a [consultation on minimum performance standards](https://energy.ec.europa.eu/news/minimum-performance-standards-data-centres-europe-public-consultation-launched-2026-09-21_en), running until 14 December, with a legislative proposal planned for the second quarter of 2027.

## Why now

The Commission relies on the International Energy Agency's projection that data centres will exceed 3% of EU electricity demand by 2030, and on its objective of tripling installed capacity within five to seven years. It also estimates that reusing half of their waste heat could heat some four million households ([European Commission](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_1667)). The problem is not only how much energy is used, but where: pressure concentrates on specific grid nodes and, in dry regions, on water.

## The case for

The argument for the label is low-cost transparency. It reuses what is already reported and turns scattered data into a comparable signal for investors, customers, grid operators and public authorities. It prevents each Member State from inventing its own scale. And it lays the technical groundwork for mandatory minimums: it is hard to set a floor without first measuring the whole fleet.

## What the label does not capture

The criticisms are mainly methodological. PUE measures how much the facility consumes on top of the servers, not whether those servers do useful work efficiently. A data centre with an excellent PUE may host obsolete or underused equipment.

The second criticism is climate. Cooling in Finland does not cost the same as in Andalusia. The label shows cooling degree days as context, but the rating is not adjusted for location, and parts of the industry fear this will distort comparisons. Energy and water also tend to move in opposite directions: evaporative cooling improves PUE at the cost of more water, while dry cooling does the reverse.

The third is data reliability. Labels are generated from information reported by operators, and the proposal leaves the possible introduction of certification or auditing to the review due by the end of 2028 ([Latham & Watkins](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/european-commission-proposes-mandatory-sustainability-label-for-data-centres)).

## The Spanish angle: from label to grid connection permit

Spain offers an early sign of how the label may be used. On 27 August the Ministry for the Ecological Transition and the Demographic Challenge (MITECO) put out for [public consultation](https://www.miteco.gob.es/en/energia/participacion/2026/detalle-participacion-publica-k-851.html) (in Spanish) a draft royal decree, a government regulation, on energy, environmental, resilience and digital sovereignty requirements for data centres. To obtain and keep grid access, new data centres of 1 MW or more would have to cover at least 80% of their consumption with renewables through self-consumption or long-term contracts, with hourly matching, and meet a PUE of 1.15 and a WUE of 0.1, that is, class A of the future EU label ([Garrigues](https://www.garrigues.com/es_ES/noticia/gobierno-pone-marcha-regulacion-centros-datos), in Spanish). Non-compliance would bring surcharges on network tariffs and, in serious cases, loss of the permit.

The context explains the ambition. According to the press, grid access granted to data centres exceeds 12 GW in Spain, 3.6 GW of it in Aragon, three times that autonomous community's electricity demand, and the consultation received more than 600 submissions ([Aragón Digital](https://www.aragondigital.es/articulo/economia/gobierno-quiere-evitar-subidas-factura-luz-decreto-centros-datos/202609111354421007504.html), in Spanish).

On paper, the fit with the EU framework is good: the decree uses the EU indicator and scale rather than creating its own. In practice, it raises two questions. First, whether requiring an A from every new project, without climate adjustment, penalises the warmer locations of southern Spain, precisely where solar power is abundant. Second, how national requirements will coexist with the EU minimums the Commission will propose in 2027: if these bring full harmonisation, the national margin could narrow.

## Assessment

My reading is that the label is a useful, low-cost step, but not a steering instrument in itself. Its value will depend on uses that are not in the delegated regulation, such as serving as a threshold for grid connection permits, which Spain has already anticipated, or as the basis for mandatory minimums. The risk is that a metric designed to inform ends up deciding who connects to the grid before its limitations have been addressed.
