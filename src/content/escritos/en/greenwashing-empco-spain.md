From 27 September, a product can no longer be marketed across the EU as "eco-friendly" or "climate neutral" without being able to prove it. Spain is one of twenty Member States that have not yet transposed the directive into national law, and already has an infringement procedure open against it. The question is not only whether Brussels will act, but what protection Spanish consumers have in the meantime.

## What changes on 27 September

[Directive (EU) 2024/825](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2024/825/oj/eng), adopted on 28 February 2024 and published in the Official Journal on 6 March, amends two well-established pieces of EU law: the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (2005/29/EC) and the Consumer Rights Directive (2011/83/EU). Its working name, empowering consumers for the green transition (hence the acronym EmpCo), sums up the goal: making sure that a product labelled sustainable actually is.

In practice, it bans three kinds of practice. First, generic environmental claims — "green", "eco-friendly", "planet-friendly" — when they cannot be backed by recognised excellent environmental performance, for instance certified under the EU Ecolabel. Second, claims of climate neutrality based on buying carbon credits outside a product's own value chain, rather than on an actual reduction in emissions. Third, sustainability labels — trust marks, logos, seals — that do not come from a certification scheme verified by an independent third party or from a public authority. The directive also introduces new durability and repairability obligations: mandatory pre-contractual information and a harmonised label for commercial durability guarantees longer than two years.

Member States had until 27 March 2026 to transpose it. Effective application begins on 27 September, with the new rules covering every business-to-consumer communication from day one — no transitional period for labels or packaging already manufactured.

## The case for, and the case that does not convince

The institutional case is one of internal-market coherence: if any trader can call a product "green" on the strength of the word alone, consumers who genuinely want to pay for sustainability have no way of telling it apart, and businesses that invest in real decarbonisation compete at a disadvantage against those that only invest in messaging. Folding these bans into the blacklist in Annex I of the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive gives national authorities a tool they can apply directly, without a case-by-case test.

Criticism, where it has surfaced, has targeted less the substance of EmpCo — adopted with broad consensus in 2024 — than the compliance cost for small businesses, the same argument that has shaped much of this mandate's simplification agenda. The difference is that here the Commission has not backed down: the directive still stands, and its application date has not moved.

## The rule that was sacrificed instead

The contrast with the proposed Green Claims Directive is instructive. Tabled by the Commission in March 2023, it went further than EmpCo: it required prior verification, by an independent third party, of virtually any environmental claim before publication. On 20 June 2025 the Commission announced its intention to withdraw it, after the European People's Party wrote asking for exactly that and Italy broke the Council consensus, arguing the text under negotiation would end up covering some 30 million microenterprises ([European Parliament, Legislative Train Schedule](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/legislative-train/theme-a-european-green-deal/file-consumers-in-the-green-transition); [European Economic and Social Committee](https://www.eesc.europa.eu/en/news-media/articles/current-affairs-withdrawal-green-claims-directive)).

The outcome is asymmetric: the more demanding rule on verifying environmental claims died at proposal stage, while the one that was already binding law — EmpCo — has proceeded untouched. In this case, simplification did not mean rolling back what had already been adopted, but giving up on going any further.

## The Spanish case

In Spain, transposition rests on the draft Sustainable Consumption Law (Anteproyecto de Ley de Consumo Sostenible), led by the Ministry of Social Rights, Consumer Affairs and the 2030 Agenda. The text went through public consultation between 4 July and 31 August 2025 and adds national measures on top of the directive itself, such as restrictions on advertising fossil fuels or short flights where a more sustainable alternative exists. Since then it has not been sent to Parliament as a bill, nor published in the Official State Gazette (BOE — Spain's equivalent of the EU's Official Journal).

On 28 May 2026 the European Commission decided to open infringement proceedings against twenty Member States — Spain among them, alongside France, the Netherlands, Poland, Portugal and others — for failing to notify complete transposition of the directive ([European Commission, INF/26/1097](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/inf_26_1097)). The letter of formal notice gave two months to respond; as of this analysis, the Commission has not made public any further decision on Spain's case, and the next step, absent a satisfactory response, would be a reasoned opinion.

The practical consequence of the delay is more nuanced than it looks. As a directive rather than a regulation, its provisions do not take effect between private parties on their own: a Spanish consumer will not be able to invoke the new, specific Annex I bans directly against a trader if Spanish law has not incorporated them. On paper, the general framework against misleading practices — already transposed since 2005/29/EC — remains in force and allows action against blatant cases through a case-by-case test. In practice, Spain will reach 27 September without the sharper tools EmpCo hands to its EU neighbours.

Having a demanding EU rule against greenwashing on the books does not solve the problem in Spain if it never reaches courts and consumer authorities equipped with the specific tools to apply it. That will depend on whether the draft Sustainable Consumption Law moves out of draft form before the Commission decides on its next step.
